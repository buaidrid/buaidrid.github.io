Beginning With Why

Much of my work begins with a simple question: Why? Why did a process fail? Why does a person behave that way? Why does a control exist? Why does a theory explain what should happen but not what actually happens?

Finding an answer usually leads to another question: What does it mean, what can be done with it, what did we miss, and what should we investigate next?

Systems Before the Name

I came to think of myself as a systems analyst relatively late. The instinct itself appeared much earlier.

As a child, I drew maps, designed games, built fictional worlds, and connected stories and settings into larger imagined systems. I was interested in how pieces fit together and what happened when one part of a system changed.

That same instinct eventually appeared in very different forms: psychology, business, data analysis, fraud detection, operational controls, process improvement, independent research, and writing.

Recurring pattern:

Understand the process producing an outcome, identify where it succeeds or fails, and determine what should happen next.

A Nonlinear Education

My education did not follow a straight path.

I originally planned to become a sign language interpreter. When that program disappeared, I moved into accounting and business. I was capable of the work, but eventually realized that being good at something did not necessarily mean I found it interesting.

Psychology changed the direction of my education. I became interested in cognition, identity, personality, behavior, and the processes that lead people toward particular outcomes.

My early interest in counseling was already process-oriented. I was especially interested in prevention. Instead of waiting until a harmful pattern had fully developed, I wanted to understand the earlier stages that produced it and whether intervention could happen sooner.

I completed my bachelor's degree in psychology at the University of Akron. Years later, I returned to school for an MBA and eventually a graduate degree in data science through the University of Phoenix.

Those fields may look disconnected at first glance. For me, they became different ways of looking at systems: people, organizations, information, incentives, decisions, and outcomes.

Finding the Process

One of the clearest examples came while working at Goodyear through InnoSource.

I became responsible for a recurring reporting process that required roughly eight hours of work. I learned how the reports were built, identified the repetitive steps, and redesigned the workflow using macros, programming, and process changes.

The work was eventually reduced to roughly thirty minutes.

I also worked on standard operating procedures and developed a practical way to move information between systems that did not communicate cleanly with one another.

That experience made something increasingly obvious. I enjoyed learning how a process worked, locating unnecessary friction or failure points, and rebuilding it more than I enjoyed simply operating inside it.
