Beginning With Why

Much of my work begins with a simple question: Why? Why did a process fail? Why does a person behave that way? Why does a control exist? Why does a theory explain what should happen but not what actually happens?

Finding an answer usually leads to another question: What does it mean, what can be done with it, what did we miss, and what should we investigate next?

Systems Before the Name

I came to think of myself as a systems analyst relatively late. The instinct itself appeared much earlier.

As a child, I drew maps, designed games, built fictional worlds, and connected stories and settings into larger imagined systems. I was interested in how pieces fit together and what happened when one part of a system changed.

That same instinct eventually appeared in very different forms: psychology, business, data analysis, fraud detection, operational controls, process improvement, independent research, and writing.

Recurring pattern:

Understand the process producing an outcome, identify where it succeeds or fails, and determine what should happen next.

A Nonlinear Education

My education did not follow a straight path.

I originally planned to become a sign language interpreter. When that program disappeared, I moved into accounting and business. I was capable of the work, but eventually realized that being good at something did not necessarily mean I found it interesting.

Psychology changed the direction of my education. I became interested in cognition, identity, personality, behavior, and the processes that lead people toward particular outcomes.

My early interest in counseling was already process-oriented. I was especially interested in prevention. Instead of waiting until a harmful pattern had fully developed, I wanted to understand the earlier stages that produced it and whether intervention could happen sooner.

I completed my bachelor's degree in psychology at the University of Akron. Years later, I returned to school for an MBA and eventually a graduate degree in data science through the University of Phoenix.

Those fields may look disconnected at first glance. For me, they became different ways of looking at systems: people, organizations, information, incentives, decisions, and outcomes.

Finding the Process

One of the clearest examples came while working at Goodyear through InnoSource.

I became responsible for a recurring reporting process that required roughly eight hours of work. I learned how the reports were built, identified the repetitive steps, and redesigned the workflow using macros, programming, and process changes.

The work was eventually reduced to roughly thirty minutes.

I also worked on standard operating procedures and developed a practical way to move information between systems that did not communicate cleanly with one another.

That experience made something increasingly obvious. I enjoyed learning how a process worked, locating unnecessary friction or failure points, and rebuilding it more than I enjoyed simply operating inside it.

Banking, Fraud, and Controls

Banking added another layer.

Working in regulated financial environments gave me direct exposure to operational controls, transaction review, fraud indicators, documentation, customer behavior, reconciliation, verification, and risk escalation.

Fraud work was especially interesting because it brought several parts of my background together.

Behavior

Psychology helped me pay attention to unusual behavior, inconsistencies, reactions, and decision patterns.

Transactions

Banking provided a structured environment where unusual activity could be examined through transactions, documentation, and account behavior.

Patterns

Data and analytical training encouraged me to look beyond individual events and search for relationships among them.

Process

Systems analysis provided the final question: how did the sequence of events make this outcome possible?

Systems and Human Fallibility

My approach to controls begins with a basic premise: people make mistakes.

Acknowledging human fallibility is not enough. Where reasonably possible, a system should reduce unnecessary opportunities for error instead of relying entirely on the person performing perfectly every time.

The same weakness that allows an honest mistake may also create an opportunity for deliberate misconduct.

Individual accountability and process accountability can coexist.

A person can be responsible for an error while the organization can still ask whether the process could have been designed in a way that made the error less likely.

That distinction matters to me.

If someone had the information, training, authority, and opportunity to act correctly and chose not to, that is different from a failure caused by missing training, poor process design, unclear instructions, or a malfunctioning system.

Good controls should consider both.

Understanding the Why

The same principle shapes how I think about training.

I do not want someone merely to memorize which button to press. Good training should help a person understand what to do, how to do it, and why the process works that way.

Understanding why gives someone more than procedural memory. It gives them a way to reason when reality does not perfectly resemble the example used during training.

Leadership Without the Ladder

I consider myself a leader, but I do not define leadership primarily through management hierarchy.

I have trained employees, developed procedures, taken on responsibilities beyond my formal title, supported operations during leadership gaps, and repeatedly stepped into problems because they needed to be solved.

Given the choice, I would rather lead a project, investigation, process, analytical function, or system than spend most of my time managing people.

Advancement interests me when it creates the ability to solve larger problems or improve more important systems.

Status by itself is less interesting.

I want to be useful.

Responsibility matters most when it provides the ability to understand, improve, or protect something that matters.

Research Beginning With Why

My independent research developed gradually from questions that kept accumulating.

I became increasingly interested in human behavior, cognitive processes, control failure, interpretation, belief, organizational systems, and the gap between how processes are expected to work and how they actually work.

Artificial intelligence later became a tool for organizing years of questions, notes, developing frameworks, and connections among projects. That work eventually became a structured Research Archive containing research programs, methodologies, literature tracking, source provenance, unresolved boundaries, and developing theoretical frameworks.

One research manuscript progressed to academic peer review. The feedback identified promise in the conceptual framework while also pointing toward the need for stronger empirical grounding, operational precision, differentiation from existing theory, and deeper engagement with competing explanations.

That response reinforced something I consider central to research: the purpose is not to protect an idea.

The purpose is to understand the problem better.

When the Line Reaches a Dead End

A failed hypothesis is not automatically wasted research.

If an expected result does not occur, the next question is not simply whether to discard the project.

It is worth asking:

  • What exactly failed?
  • Why did we expect something different?
  • Which assumption produced that expectation?
  • What actually happened instead?
  • What would another point of view see?
  • Did we overlook another variable or mechanism?
  • Does the result matter somewhere else?
  • What question exists now that did not exist before?

I tend to visualize this as a line reaching a dead end and a pen immediately changing direction.

The original research path may genuinely end. That does not mean inquiry has to end with it.

Faith, Evidence, and Questions

Christian faith is an important part of how I understand my life and my responsibility to truth.

Faith has never meant avoiding questions.

I question almost everything, including claims presented with confidence or authority. There are foundational Christian beliefs I will not compromise, but that does not mean I believe questions should be forbidden.

Evidence has changed my thinking many times. More often than complete reversal, investigation produces refinement. A belief may begin as a simple proposition and become more qualified, more precise, and more carefully bounded after examination.

I have also learned to distinguish evidence from claims made about evidence.

The responsibility to ask why applies in both directions.

Ability Does Not Determine Direction

One lesson has followed me through several careers: being good at something does not mean I want it to define my future.

I have been successful in customer service, banking, training, leadership, and other responsibilities that I would not necessarily choose as the center of my professional life.

Competence answers one question:

Can I do this?

Career direction requires another:

Do I want this to be what I do?

The work that engages me most involves pattern recognition, investigation, systems analysis, root-cause analysis, problem solving, research, data analysis, control analysis, and process improvement.

A Life Larger Than a Career

I would remain a student throughout my life if doing so were practical.

Learning is not simply a path toward employment. Neither is research. Neither is writing.

I continue to develop large fictional worlds alongside professional and academic research. In some ways that returns to the beginning: building systems, drawing connections, asking what happens when one part changes, and seeing where the next question leads.

I do not want a career that consumes everything else I care about.

I want work that is difficult enough to challenge me, analytical enough to engage me, and useful enough to matter, while still leaving room for writing, research, learning, faith, relationships, and the rest of life.

The Question After the Answer

There may never be one final subject that brings all of these interests to an end.

That may be the point.

A question leads to an investigation. An investigation produces an answer. The answer produces another question.

And when the line reaches a dead end, the pen can change direction.
