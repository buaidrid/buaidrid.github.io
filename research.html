Research Program

Interdisciplinary research examining the processes behind human behavior, decisions, controls, organizations, and outcomes, with particular attention to mechanism, effectiveness, failure, and the questions that emerge from what the research reveals.

Research Orientation

My research usually begins with an observed problem or a question rather than with a commitment to a single academic discipline.

The subjects can be quite different, but the underlying approach repeatedly returns to process.

What outcome is occurring? What process produces it? What happens between the visible stages? What conditions influence the process? Where can it fail or change? Does the explanation hold under realistic conditions? And if we understand the process, what can we do with that understanding?

Research cycle:

Process → Mechanism → Effectiveness → Intervention → Evaluation → Next Question

The cycle is not necessarily linear. Research can branch, narrow, return to an earlier assumption, or change direction when the evidence does not support the expected result.

Research Programs

The research is currently organized into five major programs. These programs provide structure without requiring related projects to become parts of a single theory.

1. Human Effectiveness & Cognitive Systems

Research into cognitive resource allocation, attention, coordination, integration, relevance, environmental competition, functional interference, cognitive variability, and the conditions affecting effective human performance.

This program asks how human cognitive systems operate under changing demands and what happens when multiple demands compete for limited processing resources.

Explore CLRM

2. Control Effectiveness & Operational Systems

Research into operational controls, human variability, verification, automaticity, control failure, structural confirmation, risk-based friction, fraud, governance, accountability, and system design.

A central concern is the difference between a control that exists procedurally and a control that remains effective under the conditions in which people actually perform the work.

Explore Control Effectiveness

3. Belief, Interpretation & Decision Processes

Research into ambiguity, interpretation, belief formation, cognitive investment, rigidity, relevance selection, belief revision, conceptual translation, external influence, social cognition, and decision processes.

This program examines how information becomes meaningful, how interpretations stabilize, and what makes beliefs easier or harder to reconsider when new information appears.

4. Meaning, Motivation & Organizations

Research into meaning, motivation, recognition, ritual, organizational behavior, policy, semantic change, institutional practice, employee engagement, and the relationship between intended purpose and experienced meaning.

The program is particularly interested in what happens when organizational practices continue after their original purpose becomes unclear, weakened, or disconnected from the people expected to participate in them.

5. Narrative & Humanities

Research using narrative, literature, philosophy, language, adaptation, history, and bounded fictional systems as analytical environments.

Current areas of interest include Shakespeare, Buffy, Babylon 5, psychology, philosophy, leadership, language, meaning, and narrative interpretation.

Narrative research remains analytically distinct from empirical research even when the two produce useful conceptual connections.

Connections Across Programs

Some questions naturally cross program boundaries.

Cognitive constraints can affect operational controls. Interpretation can influence organizational behavior. Meaning can affect motivation. Control design can depend on assumptions about human attention, memory, judgment, and behavior.

These relationships are useful, but similarity does not automatically mean two projects belong to the same theory.

The research therefore tracks relationships among projects while preserving meaningful distinctions between them.

Research Approach

Understand

Define the observed outcome and reconstruct the process that could produce it. Identify intermediate stages rather than treating the space between inputs and outcomes as a black box. Determine what mechanisms, conditions, transitions, and assumptions operate at each stage.

Challenge

Examine existing literature, competing explanations, contradictory evidence, alternative mechanisms, boundary conditions, and weaknesses in the proposed explanation.

Evaluate

Determine what evidence would distinguish among explanations, how important concepts can be operationalized, and whether predictions or interventions survive realistic testing.

Interpret Failure

When an expected result does not occur, examine what failed, which assumption produced the expectation, what happened instead, and whether the result reveals another mechanism or research direction.

Apply

Where the research supports practical application, ask what intervention, redesign, control, policy, training method, or other response follows from the explanation.

Ask the Next Question

Treat the result as a new source of information. Determine what remains unexplained, what became visible because of the investigation, and what question should be pursued next.

Productive Failure

Research is not valuable only when the original hypothesis succeeds.

A failed prediction may expose an incorrect assumption. An unsuccessful intervention may reveal that the proposed mechanism was incomplete. Contradictory evidence may establish a boundary condition. A project may even fail to answer its original question while revealing an entirely different problem worth investigating.

The intended outcome should not obscure the actual outcome.

When a research line reaches a dead end, the next task is to understand why it ended there and whether the result points somewhere else.

From Explanation to Effectiveness

Understanding why something happens is often only part of the problem.

Where research has practical implications, the next question is what should be done with that understanding.

If the research suggests a control, intervention, redesign, policy, training method, or other application, that application creates another research question:

Does it actually work?

Implementation alone does not establish effectiveness. The intervention should be evaluated under realistic conditions, including possible unintended consequences and failure modes.

The archive tracks research objects, methodologies, literature, relationships among projects, source provenance, unresolved boundaries, missing sources, historical terminology, revisions, and research paths that were later changed or abandoned.

This separation allows developing work to remain exploratory without requiring every idea to be treated as a public conclusion.

Research Principles

Conceptual Is Not Empirical

A well-developed framework is not automatically a well-supported framework. Conceptual development and empirical support should be evaluated separately.

Evidence Should Challenge

Literature should not be collected only to support an idea. Competing theories, contradictory evidence, and alternative explanations are part of the research process.

Preserve the Path

Earlier terminology, failed hypotheses, revisions, and abandoned approaches can remain important for understanding how a research idea developed.

Do Not Force Connections

Related projects can share mechanisms, methods, or questions without being collapsed into a single framework before the evidence supports doing so.

Explanation Is Not the Endpoint

Understanding a mechanism creates additional questions. Where appropriate, research should ask whether that understanding supports an intervention, redesign, framework, control, or other practical response, and then evaluate whether that response actually works.

Follow the Next Question

A useful result may resolve one question while revealing another. Research should preserve what the investigation made newly visible, including questions created by success, contradiction, or failure.
